# POLY

A filled-polygon 3D engine for the ZX Spectrum Next, in the spirit of 3D Starstrike II on the
Amstrad CPC: solid faces, hidden surfaces removed, dithered colours. The demo starts you in an
asteroid field with a planet in the distance. You fly to the planet, down through its sky to a city
of about 600 buildings, across the rooftops, then up and out again. The autopilot flies the whole
loop; the keys are ignored. It's written in NTC BASIC, with the engine in Z80 in one class
(`E3.cls`): the camera, the polygon filler, the projection, the clippers, the objects, the planet,
the city and the text.

## How it's built

### The idea behind the structure

`Main.bas` is small: it runs the autopilot, turns and moves the camera, and calls
the engine once for each part of the picture. Everything heavy is in `E3.cls`. The Subs BASIC calls
are thin wrappers; the work is in one asm block, `Lib`, so all its routines can call each other with
plain `call` and `ret`. `Lib` is called once by `Init` so it's linked, and does nothing:

```asm
' Lib: the Z80 the rest calls (called once by Init so it's linked: it does nothing)
Private Sub Lib()
#asm
            ret
```

Each wrapper swaps to the machine stack, calls into `Lib` and swaps back:

```asm
Public Sub City()
#asm
            ld ({sps}), sp
            ld sp, (ntc_csp)
            ld ({six}), ix
            push iy
            call fn_e3_lib_city
            pop iy
            ld ix, ({six})
            ld sp, ({sps})
#endasm
End Sub
```

There are four kinds of thing in the world, and each is drawn its own way:

| Part | How it's drawn | Its data |
|---|---|---|
| The stars | a pixel each | 96 directions in `E3Data.Stars()` |
| The planet | a disc from 32 directions round the view cone, or close in, the ground under the horizon | its middle, radius and ramp in `E3.ppos`, `prad`, `pcol` |
| The city | its ground, then the buildings near the camera, far to near, with less work the farther they are | `E3Data.CitySite()`, `CityMap()`, `CityBlocks()` |
| The asteroids | objects: a model turned and moved by self-modifying code, sorted far to near | `E3.obj()` (28 of them), models in `E3Data.ModelData()` |

Every shape is convex and things are drawn far to near, so there's no depth buffer.

### The classes

| File | What it does |
|---|---|
| `Main.bas` | the demo: setting up the asteroids and the autopilot's way, the flight model, the autopilot, the panel |
| `E3.cls` | the engine: the screen, the camera, the filler, the projection, the 3D and 2D clippers, objects and the scene, the planet, the city, the stars, text, `Aim` |
| `E3Data.cls` | generated by `tools/gen_data.py`: the palette, sines, the reciprocal tables, the city, the font, the stars, the models |

### The main loop

```basic
E3.Init
SetUp
Do
  Fly
  E3.CamTurn pt, yw, rl
  E3.CamMove speed
  ' the sky: black in space, blue lower down (from the last frame's height)
  alt = E3.palt
  sky = 0
  If alt < 24000 Then sky = 4 * 16 + (24000 - alt) \ 2400
  If sky > 4 * 16 + 9 Then sky = 4 * 16 + 9
  E3.Begin sky
  If sky < 4 * 16 + 3 Then E3.Stars
  E3.Planet
  E3.City
  E3.Scene
  Panel
  Frame
  E3.Flip
  frames = frames + 1
Loop
```

`SetUp` puts down the 28 asteroids, the planet and the camera, and the autopilot's 7 way points:

```basic
For k = 0 To NA - 1
  sc = 1 + Rnd(3) : kd = k And 3
  E3.ObjSet k, Rnd(10000) - 5000, Rnd(5000) - 2500, Rnd(14000), kd, sc, 130 * Pow2(sc), Rnd(5) - 2, Rnd(5) - 2, 8 + 16 * (kd And 1)
Next
```

Each asteroid is one of the 4 kinds, size 1-3 (a model unit is 2^size world units), radius 130 x
2^size, spinning by -2 to 2 each frame about two axes, and a dot of grey or brown when it's tiny.

### The frame

The sky colour comes from the height over the planet (`E3.palt`, worked out by the last frame's
`Planet`): black over 24,000, then shades 0-9 of the sky ramp (4) on the way down. `Begin` fills the
hidden buffer with it. The stars are drawn only while the sky is still dark. Then the planet, the
city and the asteroids, each painting over the last, then the panel text. `Frame` waits for the
frame and `Flip` shows the buffer just drawn.

`Begin` clears the 48K buffer with the zxnDMA, 16K at a time through slots 6 and 7:

```asm
bg_bank:
            ld a, e
            db #ED, #92, #56
            inc a
            db #ED, #92, #57
            push bc
            ld hl, bg_prog
            ld bc, 14 * 256 + #6B
            otir
            pop bc
            inc e
            inc e
            djnz bg_bank
            ld a, 10
            db #ED, #92, #56
            ld a, 11
            db #ED, #92, #57
            jr bg_end
bg_prog:
            db #83, #7D
            dw bg_src
            dw #4000
            db #24, #10, #AD
            dw #C000
            db #82, #CF, #87
bg_src:     db 0
```

`db #ED, #92, #56` is NextReg #56 = A (slot 6's page). The 14-byte DMA program is sent to port #6B
with one `otir`; it fills 16K (#4000) at #C000 from the one byte at `bg_src`, the colour.

`Flip`:

```basic
Public Sub Flip()
  Dim t As Integer
  Nx.Reg &H12, back
  t = front : front = back : back = t
End Sub
```

64 objects of 16 bytes, put there by `ObjSet`:

| Bytes | Meaning |
|---|---|
| 0-5 | x, y, z (world units) |
| 6, 7 | `oa`, `ob`: how it's turned now |

#### The models: `tools/gen_data.py`

A model is int8 points and convex faces, written one after another into `ModelData()`:

```python
# a model: vertices (int8 x, y, z), faces (ramp, normal int8 x3, vertex indices clockwise seen
# from outside). Written: nv, nf, radius, verts, then each face: n, ramp, nx, ny, nz, d (16 bit:
# n . v for its vertices), indices
```

| Bytes | Meaning |
|---|---|
| 0, 1, 2 | points, faces, radius |
| 3 points each | x, y, z |
| each face | n (corners), ramp, the normal x, y, z (x 120), d (16 bits: the plane is n . v = d), n point numbers |

So a face is n + 7 bytes, and the code steps to the next with `ld a, (iy + 0) / add a, 7`.

There are 8 models: 4 asteroids (an icosahedron's 12 points pushed out to 90-125 at random, its
convex hull's triangles found by brute force: 20 faces), and their 4 far versions (an octahedron, 6
points and 8 faces). `ModelTab()` gives each of the 4 kinds its near and far model. The asteroids
are ramps 0 (grey) and 1 (brown rock).

#### The palette

`gen_data.py` makes 16 ramps of 16 shades, dark to light, as 9-bit colours:

| Ramp | | Ramp | |
|---|---|---|---|
| 0 | grey (rock, stars; 0 is black) | 8 | sandstone |
| 1 | brown rock | 9 | red / fire |
| 2 | the planet's ocean | 10 | haze |
| 3 | the planet's land | 11 | violet |
| 4 | sky | 12 | gold / lit windows |
| 5 | ground | 13 | teal |
| 6 | concrete | 14 | dusk |
| 7 | glass | 15 | the panel's green |

A colour is `ramp * 16 + shade`. Lit faces get a level 0-30: the pair is shade `level / 2` and,
when the level is odd, the next shade up, so 16 shades and the 15 mixes between them.

#### The world and the city

The planet's middle is (157, -352, 1172) x 256 world units and its radius 102,400 (`PlanetR` 400,
in 256s). The city sits on the planet where it faces the start, with its own frame: east, up and
north.

`CitySite()`:

| Bytes | Meaning |
|---|---|
| 0-8 | the site, 24 bits each way |
| 9-26 | east, up, north (s1.14) |
| 27-58 | 8 kinds of building: half width, half depth, height / 4, ramp |

The kinds (`TYPES`):

| Kind | Half width | Half depth | Height / 4 | Ramp |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 0 | 40 | 40 | 15 | concrete |
| 1 | 48 | 36 | 30 | glass |
| 2 | 36 | 48 | 45 | sandstone |
| 3 | 52 | 52 | 22 | concrete |
| 4 | 32 | 32 | 70 | glass |
| 5 | 44 | 44 | 55 | teal |
| 6 | 56 | 40 | 12 | red |
| 7 | 40 | 56 | 100 | glass |

`CityMap()` is 48 x 48 cells of 128 units, a byte each: 0 none, else 1 + the kind. Every 4th row and

0.15 + 0.35 r, r the distance from the middle), with tall kinds in the middle and low ones outside.

`CityBlocks()` is the 12 x 12 blocks between the roads (3 x 3 cells each): their buildings' mean
height (x 0.9, at least 4; 0 for a block with fewer than 2 buildings) and their commonest ramp. These
are the far view's boxes.

#### `vpage` and `ffpage`

Two 256-byte pages in `Lib`, aligned to 256:

```asm
            ALIGN 256
vpage:      ds 256                  ; per vertex i: +i its code, +64 + i its x, +128 + i its y (bytes,
                                    ; in the fast range)
ffpage:     ds 256                  ; ff_v: the fast fill's points (x, y bytes; up to 128)
```

Point i's code is at `vpage + i`: 0 when it's on the screen (x 1-255, y 0-192), else bit 7 set
(with `vcode`'s bits when it's outside the view). Its x and y as bytes are at `vpage + 64 + i` and
`vpage + 128 + i`. With H = `vpage >> 8` and L = i, `or #40` gets to x and `xor #C0` from there to y:

```asm
            ld a, (hl)
            inc hl
            push hl
            or #40
            ld l, a
            ld h, vpage >> 8
            ld a, (hl)
            ld (ix + 0), a
            ld a, l
            xor #C0
            ld l, a
            ld a, (hl)
            ld (ix + 1), a
```

`ffpage` (`ff_v`) is the polygon the fast filler works on: x, y byte pairs. Because the page is
aligned, going round the points is just changing L.

### The camera

`cm` holds the camera's right, up and forward rows. `CamTurn p, y, r` turns about its own axes by
small angles (p / 16384 radians) with `rotp`, which uses s = a and c = 1 - a^2 / 2:

```asm
; rotp: rows IX (A) and IY (B) of the camera turned by HL / 16384 radians: A = A c + B s,
; B = B c - A s (s = a, c = 1 - a^2 / 2)
```

Pitch turns F toward U, yaw F toward R, roll U toward R. Then `renorm` squares the camera up again:
F made unit length, U made square to F and unit length, R = U x F. The unit step doesn't divide:
it scales by (3 - |v|^2) / 2, which is enough for a vector that's already close to 1:

```asm
rn_unit:
            push ix
            pop iy
            call mrow               ; |v|^2 (s1.14)
            ex de, hl
            ld hl, 49152
            or a
            sbc hl, de
            srl h
            rr l                    ; (3 - k) / 2
```

`renorm` also works out, once a frame, what the rest needs from the camera: the sun in the view
(`suc`), the camera shifted right by 0-6 (`cms`, 7 x 18 bytes, rounded: one for each object size),
and 8-bit copies of the camera and the sun (`cm8`, `suc8`, `sunw8`, x 127) for the cheap paths.

`CamMove s` adds F x s / 16 to `cp`, which is 24.8 in 32 bits so slow speeds still move.

### The polygon filler

`fill` takes `pvx`, `pvy`. If every point is on the screen (x 1-255, y 0-192) it copies them into
`ff_v` as bytes, finds the top and bottom, and goes straight to the fast way (`ff`). If not, it works
out which sides the points cross (`fl_slow`): all off one side is nothing; else `clip2` cuts the
polygon to just those sides, then the fast way.

#### The fast way: both edges in registers

The left edge is in HL' (x, 8.8) with its step in DE'; the right edge in IX with its step in BC'.
IYH is the row's address (high byte) and IYL the rows to go. An edge's step comes from `edge88`: the
dx times 65536 / dy from `REdge()`, two `mul d, e`s:

```asm
            ld e, (hl)
            inc hl
            ld d, (hl)              ; R = 65536 / dy
            ld a, d
            ld d, c
            mul d, e                ; |dx| R low
            ld l, d
            ld e, a
            ld d, c
            mul d, e                ; |dx| R high
            ld h, 0
            add hl, de              ; |the step| (8.8)
```

The polygon is done in chunks: up to the next corner on either side, or the end of the 64-row
window, whichever comes first. A window is 64 rows of 256 bytes: 16K, two 8K pages of the back
buffer mapped into slots 6 and 7 (#C000-#FFFF). When a corner is reached, `ff_advl` or `ff_advr`
goes round `ff_v` to the next edge (the left side backwards, the right side forwards).

Each row:

```asm
fr_row:
            exx
            ld a, h                 ; x0
            add hl, de
            db #08                  ; ex af, af
            ld a, ixh               ; x1
            add ix, bc
            exx
            ld b, a
            db #08
            ld c, a
            ld a, iyh
            ld h, a
            inc iyh
            ld a, b
            cp c
            jp c, fr_cont           ; x1 < x0: none
            bit 0, c                ; odd start: one byte (an odd pixel: D)
            jr z, fr_a
            ld l, c
            ld (hl), d
            cp c
            jp z, fr_cont
            inc c
fr_a:
            bit 0, b                ; even end: one byte (E)
            jr nz, fr_b
            ld l, b
            ld (hl), e
            ld a, b
            cp c
            jp z, fr_cont
            dec b
fr_b:
            ld a, b                 ; pairs
            sub c
            add a, 1
            rra
            ld l, b
            inc hl
            db #F9                  ; ld sp, hl
            neg
            ld l, a
            ld h, (fr_pend >> 8) - 1
            jp (hl)
            ALIGN 128
            REPT 128
            push de
            ENDR
fr_pend:
fr_cont:
            ld a, d
            ld d, e
            ld e, a
            dec iyl
            jp nz, fr_row
            jp ff_after
```

A pixel is a byte, so a `push de` writes two pixels in 11 T-states. SP is set to just past the
row's right end, and the code jumps into a run of 128 `push de`s so that exactly enough are left.
The run ends on a 256-byte boundary (`fr_pend` is #1800 in the build), so the place to jump to is
just the number of pushes negated: L = -n, H = #17. An odd pixel at the start and an even one at the
end are written alone.

At the end of each row D and E swap. That's the dither: the two colours in `pc` make a checker, and
when they're the same it's a plain colour. The README puts the cost at about 225 T-states a row plus
5.5 a pixel.

Triangles have their own setup (`ftri`), and `ffi` fills a polygon straight from point numbers in
`vpage`, so an object's faces never go through `pvx` and `pvy` when they're on the screen.

### Clipping

#### In 3D: `vcode`, `poly3`, `clip1`

`vcode` gives a point in the view a 5-bit code:

```asm
; vcode: HL = x, DE = y, BC = z in the view (under 16384 each way) -> A: bit 0 z < 16 (the near
; plane), bit 1 x > 4z, bit 2 -x > 4z, bit 3 y > 4z, bit 4 -y > 4z (a wide guard band: the 2D
; clipper cuts the rest)
```

The view is 90 degrees across (|x| < z at the screen's edge), so the side planes are at 4z: well
outside the screen. Only polygons that cross the near plane or go far off to one side are cut in 3D;
everything else is projected as it is and the 2D clipper does the screen edges.

`poly3` cuts the polygon in `cla` by each plane whose bit is in `cmask` (Sutherland-Hodgman, one
plane at a time, swapping between `cla` and `clb`), projects what's left into `pvx`, `pvy` and calls
`fill`:

```asm
p3_pl:
            ld a, (p3_m)
            srl a
            ld (p3_m), a
            jr nc, p3_nx
            call clip1
            ld a, ({cn})
            cp 3
            ret c
            ld hl, (p3_src)
            ld de, (p3_dst)
            ld (p3_src), de
            ld (p3_dst), hl
```

A point's distance inside a side plane is z - x / 4 (and the like), so testing a plane is a shift
and a subtraction (`cdist`). Where an edge crosses, t = |dp| / (|dp| + |dc|) with `udivf`.

#### On the screen: `clip2`

The same again in 2D, to x 1-255 and y 0-192, but only for the sides the polygon crosses:

```asm
c2_pass:
            ld a, (c2_bd)           ; (only the sides crossed)
            inc a
            ld b, a
            ld a, (c2_mask)
c2_mb:
            rrca
            djnz c2_mb
            jr nc, c2_skip
            call c2_one
```

Here t comes from `REdge()` instead of a divide: the sum |dp| + |dc| is halved (with |dp|) until it's
under 1024, then t = |dp| x (65536 / sum). The result goes straight into `ff_v` as bytes for the
fast way.

### The projection: `proj`

The screen x is 128 + x x 128 / z and y is 96 - y x 128 / z. There's no divide:

```asm
; proj: HL = x, DE = y, BC = z (> 0) in the view, |x| and |y| at most 2z (about) -> HL = the screen
; x, DE = the screen y. z = n 2^k (n 256..4095; x, y shifted by k too), R = 2^24 / n; then
; x R / 2^17 by three multiplies (the low bytes' product left out: under half a pixel)
```

z, x and y are shifted together until z is 256-4095, then R is looked up in `RProj()`
(3,840 entries). x x R / 2^17 is three 8 x 8 multiplies:

```asm
pm_1:
            ld b, h                 ; xh
            ld c, l                 ; xl
            ld a, (pj_rl)
            ld e, a
            ld d, b
            mul d, e                ; xh rl
            push de
            ld a, (pj_rh)
            ld e, a
            ld d, c
            mul d, e                ; xl rh
            pop hl
            add hl, de              ; m (carry: its bit 16)
            ld l, h
            ld h, 0
            rl h                    ; m >> 8
            ld a, (pj_rh)
            ld e, a
            ld d, b
            mul d, e                ; xh rh
            add hl, de
            srl h                   ; >> 1
            rr l
```

The stars use the same table and `pj_m` with z / 4.

### Objects

`Scene` goes through `obj()`. For each object it adds the spins, works out where it is in the view
the cheap way (`oprep8`: the 8-bit camera, to 1/128) and whether it's seen (`ocull`: a sphere of its
radius against the near plane and the four side planes), and puts the ones seen into a list sorted
far to near (an insertion sort on z, `sc_z` and `sc_x`). Then it draws the list.

#### Level of detail

How big an object is on the screen (128 x radius / z) picks how it's drawn:

```asm
            ex de, hl               ; HL = z: how big (128 rad / z): over 32 pixels near (exact, the
            ld b, 2                 ; near model); 16..32 far (the cheap way, the near model); 4..16
            call sc_shr             ; far (the far model); 2..4 a blob; under 2 a dot
```

`sc_shr` shifts z right and compares it with the radius, so each step is a shift and a subtraction.
"The cheap way" (`od_far`) uses the 8-bit camera for the transform and tests the faces before their
points are projected. A blob is a hexagon the object's size in its dot colour (dithered with the
next shade); a dot is 2 x 2 pixels.

#### The transform: self-modifying code from `tools/gen_xform.py`

`odraw` works out C = M O: the camera times the object's turn (about y by `oa`, then x by `ob`),
using the camera already shifted right by 6 - `osc` (`cms`) so the model's int8 units come out at
the right size. Then each of the 9 numbers of C is written into the transform code itself.

A point's coordinates go in as u = v + 128, unsigned bytes in B, C and IYL (`xor #80`). Each of the
9 terms is |C| split into its high and low bytes, each multiplied by u with `mul d, e`. This is the
code `gen_xform.py` writes for one term (row 0, column 0):

```asm
xr0c:      ld hl, 0
            ld e, b
xt00h:      ld d, 0
            mul d, e
xt00a:      nop
            nop
            add hl, de
            ld e, b
xt00l:      ld d, 0
            mul d, e
            ld e, d
            ld d, 0
xt00b:      nop
            nop
            add hl, de
```

The `ld d, 0`s get the high and low bytes of |C|. The term's sign is patched into the three bytes
at `xt00a` and `xt00b`: `00 00 19` is `nop, nop, add hl, de` and `B7 ED 52` is `or a, sbc hl, de`.
The patch writer, also from `gen_xform.py`:

```asm
            ld hl, ({ccl} + 0)
            bit 7, h
            jr z, xs00p
            ex de, hl
            ld hl, 0
            or a
            sbc hl, de
            ld a, h
            ld (xt00h + 1), a
            ld a, l
            ld (xt00l + 1), a
            ld a, #B7
            ld (xt00a), a
            ld (xt00b), a
            ld hl, #52ED
            ld (xt00a + 1), hl
            ld (xt00b + 1), hl
            jr xs00e
xs00p:
            ld a, h
            ld (xt00h + 1), a
            ld a, l
            ld (xt00l + 1), a
            xor a
            ld (xt00a), a
            ld (xt00b), a
            ld hl, #1900
            ld (xt00a + 1), hl
            ld (xt00b + 1), hl
xs00e:
```

Each row starts (`xr0c`) at the object's middle in the view less half the row's sum, which takes
out the 128 the coordinates carry:

```asm
            ; the rows' starts: c i - K i (K i = (C'(i, 0) + C'(i, 1) + C'(i, 2)) / 2: the 128 the
            ; points' coordinates carry)
```

So one point is 18 `mul d, e`s and adds, with no signs to test and nothing loaded from memory but the
point itself. The script prints the code, and it's pasted into `E3.cls` between `;@@XFORM` and `;@@XFORM-END`;
the patch writer sits after the comment that names `;@@SPLIT`.

After the transform: if the object crosses the view's edges (`ovis` 2) the point in the view is kept
in `ov` for the clipper with its `vcode`. Otherwise it's projected; on the screen, its bytes go into
`vpage` with code 0, and off the screen its code is #80.

#### Face culling

Every model is convex, so a face turned away is never seen and the rest never overlap. How a face
is tested depends on where its points are:

- **All on the screen** (the usual case): the winding of the first three points' bytes. The sizes
  are multiplied with `mul d, e` and the signs kept apart:

```asm
            ; (x1 - x0)(y2 - y0) - (y1 - y0)(x2 - x0) > 0: the sizes multiplied, the signs apart
```

- **In the view but some off the screen** (`of_w16`): the same with 16-bit points, halved together
  until the differences fit 8 bits.
- **Crossing the view's edges**: projected points can't be trusted, so it's done in 3D: seen if
  n . e > d, e being the camera in the model (`of_e`, worked out once an object, only when needed).
- **The cheap way**: before any point is projected (`od_pa`), each face is tested with n . e8 > d,
  e8 the camera in the model as bytes. The faces seen are listed (`fvis`) and their points marked
  (`vmark`), and only the marked points go through the transform.

A face whose points are all outside one plane (the AND of their codes) is left out.

#### Lighting

The sun is turned into the model once an object (`osn8`, bytes), so a face's shade is one 8-bit dot
product with its stored normal:

```asm
of_lit:
            ; the shade: 3 + n . sun (0..30: 16 shades and the steps between, dithered)
```

The level becomes the colour pair: `ramp * 16 + level / 2`, and the next shade up as the second
colour when the level is odd. Then the face goes to `ffi` (all on the screen), `fill` (16-bit points)
or `poly3` (crossing the view's edges).

### The planet

```asm
; planet: the planet drawn (ppos: its middle in the world / 256; prad: its radius / 256; pcol: its
; ramp). The view's directions touching it make a cone; 32 of them (the generators) round its edge,
; cut to the view like a face, are its outline. All in the view: the disc, lit toward the point under
; the sun (bands shrunk toward it), an atmosphere's rim; else (near: the horizon) the ground below.
; palt: the height over it (world units, to 32767)
```

The camera-to-planet vector is worked out in 24 bits and shifted until each part fits
-16384..16383 (`pl_fit`, the shift kept in `pl_k`), then turned into the view. Its length D (`len3`, with a 32-bit square root)
and the radius r give the height, `palt`, and the cone: the axis u = a / D, sin = r / D, cos =
sqrt(1 - sin^2). Two directions square to the axis, v and w, are found, and the 32 generators are
cos u + sin (cos f v + sin f w) for f = 0, 8 ... 248 (8-bit `mul8s` with `trig`). Each gets a `vcode`.
`pup` is -u: "up" from the planet in the view, which the flight model uses.

- All 32 outside one plane: not seen.
- All in the view: the disc. The generators are projected and kept (`pl_sx`, `pl_sy`). First an
  atmosphere, the outline scaled 17/16 about the middle in the haze ramp (not when `pcol` is 15 or
  the disc is over 200 pixels across). Then the disc, dark (shades 2 and 3). Then the lit side: the
  point under the sun, S = a + r sun, and smaller copies of the outline shrunk toward it, lighter
  each time (4, 3 or 2 bands by how big the disc is). If S is round the back, the bands shrink toward
  the edge point nearest the sun, and thinner the farther behind the sun is.
- Some out of the view: the camera is close and the planet fills the bottom of the screen. The 32
  generators go to `poly3` as one polygon and come out as the ground below the horizon (shades 6
  and 7 of `pcol`). The sky above it is `Begin`'s colour.

The scaled copies are all `pl_hom`: each point is `DE + (HL - DE) * f / 16`, so every band reuses
the same 32 projected points.

### The city

`city` does nothing unless the site is within 16,000 units of the camera each way. Then, once a
frame:

1. The site in the view (`cy_c0`), its east, up and north in the view, and the camera's place on
   the city (`cy_cl`: across, up, along). Under the ground: nothing.
2. Each kind's corner steps (`cy_tv`) from 8-bit copies of the frame.
3. `cy_tx` and `cy_tz`: for every column and row, that cell's offset in the view, by adding a step
   48 times (`cy_tab`). A cell's middle is then `cy_c0 + TX(i) + TZ(j)`: three adds, no multiplies.
4. The shades for the 5 face directions from the sun (`cy_lv`: 4-30).
5. The ground: one concrete quad 3,136 units each way about the site.
6. The cells within 18 of the camera each way (`cy_cell`), and the blocks they're in.

#### Blocks far away

`cy_blocks` looks at each 4 x 4-cell block in range. One whose middle is 1,100 or more away (z) is
flagged in `cy_bf`, so its cells are skipped, and listed once as kind 255 if it's in the view. It's
drawn as a box: half width 176 units each way, as high as `CityBlocks()` says.

#### The cell scan

For each cell in range with a building, not in a far block, a rough test against the view:

```asm
            ld hl, (cy_t6)          ; seen? z + 200 > 0 and |x| < z + 200 (a rough sphere)
```

Up to 120 are listed with i, j, kind and z / 64.

#### Far to near: a counting sort

The list is sorted by its 64 distances, far first, in three passes (count, starts, place):

```asm
            ld hl, cy_cnt + 63      ; the starts: far first
            ld b, 64
            ld c, 0
cs_st:
            ld a, (hl)
            ld (hl), c
            add a, c
            ld c, a
            dec hl
            djnz cs_st
```

Then each is drawn: `cy_blk` for a block, `cy_bld` for a building.

#### Which faces: no culling maths

A building is a box, so which of its 5 faces you can see depends only on where the camera is beside
it. `cy_fmask` compares the camera's offset from the building with its half width and half depth,
and its height with the building's:

```asm
cy_fmask:
            xor a
            ld (cy_fm), a
            ld hl, (cy_dx)          ; +E: dx > w; -E: dx < -w
            ld a, (cy_w)
            call cy_side
            ld (cy_fm), a
            ld hl, (cy_dz)          ; +N, -N
            ld a, (cy_dd)
            call cy_side
            add a, a
            add a, a
            ld hl, cy_fm
            or (hl)
            ld (hl), a
            ld a, (cy_h4)           ; top: the camera over it (y > 4 h)
```

At most 3 faces, and only their corners are worked out (`cy_have` remembers which of the 8 are
done).

#### Less work the farther it is

By the high byte of the building's middle z (`oc + 5`, z / 256):

| z / 256 | How |
|---|---|
| under 2 | exact: each corner moved into the view and projected (`cy_corner`) |
| 2 and 3 | affine: 4 projections (the bottom middle, and it plus each step), the corners worked out from those |
| 4 and over | one polygon: the outline of the faces seen, in the haze ramp |

The affine corners (`cy_affs`, `cy_acorner`) are just adds and subtracts on the screen:

```asm
; cy_acorner: corner (cr_k) on the screen the affine way: p0 -+ dw -+ dd (+ dh), its code by the
; screen (0 or #80: no clipping; far ones are small)
```

When the bottom middle is closer than 512 (z), the affine way would be wrong, so that building is
done exactly (`cy_bz`).

The outline comes from a table, `cy_sil`: for each mask of faces seen, how many corners and which,
in order. A box seen from a corner and above has 6 outline points, so 3 faces become one polygon:

```asm
cy_sil:                             ; each face mask's outline: how many corners, the corners
            db 0, 0, 0, 0, 0, 0, 0
            db 4, 1, 5, 7, 3, 0, 0
            db 4, 2, 6, 4, 0, 0, 0
```

Its colour is the haze ramp at the top's shade, lightened. A far block's box is drawn the same way.

A face goes to `ffi` when all 4 corners are on the screen, to `fill` with 16-bit points when they're
in the view, and to `poly3` when it crosses the view's edges.

### Stars and text

The stars are 96 directions. Each is turned by the 8-bit camera (three `mul8s` a row), dropped when
behind or outside the view, projected with `RProj` and written as one pixel in its colour. They only
move when the camera turns.

`Char x, y, c, col` draws a 5 x 7 character from `Font()` straight into the back buffer, only its set
pixels, so the text has no box behind it:

```asm
tx_px:
            sla c
            jr nc, tx_p0
            ld (hl), a
tx_p0:
            inc l
            djnz tx_px
```

`Text` (6 pixels a character) and `Num` (leading 0s as spaces) are BASIC on top of it. The panel is
the speed and height at the top and a blinking AUTOPILOT at the bottom, in the panel's green (ramp
15).

### The flight model: `Main.bas`

`Ctl.Read()` reads every key and the stick in one asm routine into bits (`Dive`, `Climb`, `RollL`,
`RollR`, `Faster`, `Slower`, `YawL`, `YawR`, `Auto`). The keys are Elite's: S dive, X climb, N and M
roll (the comma and full stop are Symbol Shift N and M, so those work too), Space faster, V slower
(/ is Symbol Shift V), and Z and C turn, which Elite doesn't have. The Kempston port reading #FF means nothing's
there and is ignored.

`Fly` turns the keys into pitch, yaw and roll (`pt`, `yw`, `rl`) and the speed. H, or 400 frames
with no keys, hands over to the autopilot; any other key takes it back.

**Now a demo (2026-10-21):** flying by hand into the planet could crash it, so `Fly` sets
`keys = 0` instead of calling `Ctl.Read()`: the autopilot always flies, and the code above for the
keys is still there (unreached) for when hand flying is fixed. Tested with the keys pressed at random
through a whole loop (asteroids, planet, city, out and back): no crash.

```basic
    ' in the air a bank turns you
    If E3.palt < 20000 Then yw = yw - E3.pup(0) \ 48
  End If
  ' slower near the ground: you come down gently; never into it
  If speed < 0 Then speed = 0
  If speed > 32000 Then speed = 32000
  If E3.palt < 15000 Then
    smax = 300 + E3.palt * 2
    If speed > smax Then speed = smax
  End If
  If E3.palt < 40 And pt < 300 Then pt = 300
```

`pup(0)` is how far "up" leans to the side in the view, so a bank becomes a turn. The top speed drops
with height under 15,000, and under 40 units you're pushed nose up.

#### The autopilot

The way is 7 points, each with a speed and how near is near enough:

```basic
Way 0, 6, 2, 20, 3000, 12
Way 1, -10, -4, 40, 3000, 12
Way 2, 104, -233, 777, 32000, 6
Way 3, 110, -247, 787, 1400, 6
Way 4, 101, -227, 795, 1200, 6
Way 5, 86, -193, 643, 24000, 30
Way 6, 0, 0, -8, 32000, 20
```

Through the asteroids, to the city, across it low (points 3 and 4), up, and back to the start.
`Autopilot` gives the engine the point and asks where it is in the view (`E3.Aim`). When it's close
(`aimk` 0 and every part under `wr * 256`) it moves on to the next point. Otherwise it doubles the
vector until its biggest part is at least 8192, and steers toward it:

```basic
  yw = vx \ 12 : pt = vy \ 12
  If vz < 0 Then
    If vx < 0 Then yw = -700 Else yw = 700
  End If
  ' over the city (its way points): hold 300 up, nose level, not at the point's height
  If wp >= 3 And wp <= 4 And E3.palt < 3000 Then
    mx = (300 - E3.palt) * 4
    If mx > 3000 Then mx = 3000
    If mx < -3000 Then mx = -3000
    pt = (mx - E3.pup(2)) \ 6
  End If
```

A point behind is a hard turn. Over the city it holds 300 units up instead of aiming at the point's
height. The roll is `pup(0) \ 12`, which keeps the wings level with the planet. The speed goes toward
the point's speed, halved while turning hard.

### Decisions made to fit / for speed

- **One `Lib` block for all the Z80**, so routines `call` each other on the machine stack. The BASIC
  Subs are a few lines each.
- **Two buffers and a DMA clear.** The picture is never seen half drawn, and the 48K clear is three
  16K DMA runs instead of a CPU loop.
- **The fill by pushes**, two pixels in 11 T-states, with the run of pushes placed so the jump
  address is the count negated.
- **Points as bytes when they're on the screen** (`vpage`, `ffpage`), aligned pages so x and y are
  found by `or #40` and `xor #C0`.
- **Clip only what crosses**: a wide guard band in 3D, then only the screen sides a polygon crosses.
- **No divides in the inner loops**: 7.5K of projection reciprocals and 2K of edge reciprocals.
- **The transform as patched code**: signs patched in, the camera pre-shifted for each object size
  (`cms`), coordinates offset by 128 so the multiplies are unsigned.
- **8-bit copies of the camera** for sorting, for far objects and for the stars.
- **The planet from 32 directions**, so one set of projected points makes the atmosphere, the disc and
  every band.
- **The city's cell offsets as tables** (`cy_tx`, `cy_tz`), its faces picked by where the camera is,
  its far buildings affine or one outline, its farthest blocks one box, and a counting sort.
- **Convex shapes only**, drawn far to near: no depth buffer.
- Everything fits in the program area with 6,621 bytes to spare. The README lists what's still open:
  object code paged in from the Next's other RAM, a quad fast path in the filler, and not drawing
  the planet's ground under the city's ground plate.

NTC rules that show in the code:

- **SP is the thread.** An asm Sub that needs `call` or `push` saves SP (`ld ({sps}), sp`), switches
  to the machine stack with `ld sp, (ntc_csp)`, and puts SP back at the end. Inside `Lib` the
  routines use the stack freely; `fill` also keeps its own copy (`fl_ssp`) because it takes SP for
  the pushes.
- **IX and IY are kept**: IX in `six`, IY pushed after the stack switch.
- **Parameters arrive as pointers**: `pop hl`, then read the value through it. A Function's result
  goes back the same way (`pop hl / ld (hl), e / inc hl / ld (hl), d`).
- **`{name}` is a class variable** in asm (`ld ({sps}), sp`, `ld hl, {pvx}`), and labels in an asm
  block become `fn_<class>_<sub>_<label>`: `call fn_e3_lib_fill`, `ld de, fn_e3data_redge_redge_d`.
- **A Function nobody calls isn't linked**, so `Init` calls every `E3Data` table once: `ta =
  E3Data.REdge() : ta = E3Data.RProj() ...` "(linked: the Z80 reads them)", and `Lib` itself is
  called once.
- **Interrupts are off** ("NTC runs with interrupts off"), so the fill can point SP anywhere. There's
  no `ei` anywhere in the engine.
- Some instructions are written as bytes: `ex af, af'` as `db #08`, `ld sp, hl` as `db #F9`, and the
  Next's NextReg write as `db #ED, #92, #56`. `mul d, e` is written as itself.
- **16-bit Integers**: world positions are 16 bits, so the camera's position is 32 bits (24.8) inside
  E3 and the planet and the way points are given in 256s. `CamAt k, a, b` sets the camera to
  `a * 256 + b` "to 8 million each way".

### Building

```
python3 tools/gen_data.py      # only after changing the tables, models or city
node test/nb.js .              # compile and assemble: build/POLY.NEX
```

`nb.js` compiles `game.json`'s files with NTC BASIC for the zxn platform with turbo on, writes
`build/POLY.asm`, and runs `sjasmplus` on it, which writes `POLY.NEX` and `POLY.sym` (the tests read
the symbols). It needs NTC BASIC's `src` folder (its second argument, or `NTC_SRC=`) and `sjasmplus`
on the PATH. `tools/gen_xform.py`'s output is pasted into `E3.cls` (see the transform above).

### How it was tested

The tests drive a headless ZEsarUX. `test/zx.py` drives a headless ZEsarUX over its remote protocol
(ZRCP): it loads a NEX, presses keys, takes screenshots and reads memory, finding variables by name in
the `.sym` file (`var_frames`, `var_e3_palt`, `var_wp`).

- `tour2.py NEX secs every` runs the autopilot loop and every few seconds stops the emulator
  (`enter-cpu-step`), takes a screenshot (`tour_NNN.png` beside the NEX), and prints the frame rate
  since the last one, the height and the way point. Stopping first means no half-drawn screenshots;
  the README says a shot taken while it runs can show a single black row, which is the emulator
  catching it mid-frame.
- `fpsv.py NEX` counts `var_frames` over 6 seconds and prints loops a second.
- `bench13.sh` copies the current `E3.cls` and `E3Data.cls` into `test/t13`, builds it, and runs
  `fpsv.py` on it. `t13` is a fixed view: the camera placed 600 units over the city looking at it, drawing only the planet and the city, so a change to the engine can be measured on the same
  picture every time.
- `prof3.py NEX [secs] [n]` turns on the emulator's CPU history, takes the last 400,000 instructions'
  addresses, finds each one's routine from the symbols, and prints the share of each part: fill rows,
  fill setup, clip2, clip3, proj, vertices, faces, cull, waiting (Frame) and so on (`DETAIL=1` lists
  the labels too).
- `pc.py` samples where the PC is; `peek.py` reads words at symbols after a few seconds; `dbg.py`
  stops at an address and steps.

The folders `t0`-`t13` and `dma` are the stages the engine was built in, each a small `Main.bas`
with its own copy of the engine: polygons of every size (`t0`-`t2`), a full-screen polygon 4 times
timed with `Nx.Raster()` (`t3`), asteroid fields (`t4`-`t6`, `t8`, `t9`), micro-benchmarks of single
routines (`t7`), the planet from a still camera (`t10`, `t11`), the city (`t13`), and the DMA fill
(`dma`).

### Lessons

- **Most polygons are on the screen.** Handle that case with bytes and registers, and only take the
  slow path (16-bit points, clipping) for the ones that need it.
- **Clip only against what's crossed**: a wide 3D guard band and a 2D clip of just the sides crossed
  keep most polygons away from the clipper.
- **Patch the code, not the data.** The object transform has its numbers and signs written into it
  once an object, so each point is straight-line multiplies and adds.
- **Let distance decide the work**: exact, cheap, a smaller model, a hexagon, a dot for objects;
  exact, affine, one outline, one box per block for buildings.
- **Use what you know about the shape.** A box's visible faces come from where the camera is beside
  it; a convex model needs no depth sort inside it.
- **Measure on a fixed picture and profile by routine.** `t13` and `prof3.py` show whether a change
  helped and where the time went.
